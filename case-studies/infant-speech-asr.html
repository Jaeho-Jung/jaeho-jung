LetsPlay Infant Speech ASR — Letting Experimental Evidence Change the Architecture

A university–industry capstone project exploring real-time voice interaction for young children. My AI work focused on a difficult constraint: recognizing short Korean child utterances with scarce, distribution-shifted data and then turning the result into a deployable system.

The objective was not simply to fine-tune a speech model. I needed to determine whether the available data supported the recognition claim, whether model improvements generalized beyond the training distribution, and whether the resulting inference path made sense inside the product.

1. Expanding scarce data without confusing augmentation with generalization

The project began with roughly 200 recorded utterances. Child speech is unusually difficult for general-purpose ASR because articulation, pitch, duration, and pronunciation can differ substantially from adult speech.

I built and evaluated an augmentation pipeline using:

  • spectrogram augmentation and swapping,
  • frequency-domain filtering,
  • background-noise mixing at controlled SNRs,
  • reverberation,
  • speed perturbation.

This expanded the experimental dataset from approximately 200 to 4,200 files. Fine-tuning Whisper-tiny on the constrained keyword dataset eventually produced extremely strong in-distribution character-error results after regularization and training changes.

I did not interpret that as proof that the system understood real child speech. With a very small keyword vocabulary and many samples derived from a limited original distribution, a near-perfect metric can be stronger evidence of task memorization than of target-domain generalization.

The experiment forced a useful separation: “Can the model learn this constrained dataset?” and “Does the evidence support the product claim on real child speech?” are different questions.

That distinction shaped the later work: target-domain evaluation mattered more than continuing to optimize a metric on the augmented training environment.

2. A successful model conversion was not a successful deployment

The original product direction favored on-device speech recognition. I tested several conversion and runtime paths to see whether the model could be moved into a mobile-friendly inference environment.

Approach Observed result Interpretation
ONNX → TFLite Conversion completed; inference emitted abnormal repeated character sequences Artifact conversion alone did not establish runtime viability
OpenVINO INT8 Conversion and CPU inference succeeded Alternative runtime path was technically viable

I did not claim a confirmed root cause for the TFLite failure. Operator support and dynamic-tensor handling were plausible contributors, but the evidence was insufficient to isolate a single cause.

More importantly, the engineering decision changed. The relevant question was no longer “Can I eventually force this model onto the device?” but “Is continuing to invest in edge deployment rational under the project deadline and recognition constraints?”

I pivoted the architecture from edge inference to server-side GPU inference. The technical preference did not get to overrule the product constraint.

3. Rejecting benchmark claims that the measurement method could not support

After the capstone, I continued the project with production-oriented inference experiments across PyTorch, ONNX Runtime, dynamic INT8 quantization, torch.compile, and Faster-Whisper / CTranslate2.

One CPU experiment produced a counter-intuitive result: under the recorded Colab CPU and implementation conditions, dynamic INT8 inference was slower than direct FP32 inference. I kept that conclusion local to the measured setup rather than generalizing it into “INT8 is bad for Whisper.”

A second measurement problem was more instructive. An early Faster-Whisper memory figure appeared dramatically smaller than the PyTorch result. But it had been collected with torch.cuda.memory_allocated(). CTranslate2 allocates CUDA memory outside PyTorch's allocator, so that number could not support a cross-engine VRAM comparison.

I removed the resulting “98% memory reduction” claim and marked the measurement for re-testing with process-level instrumentation such as NVML or nvidia-smi. I applied the same rule to latency: measurements with different warm-up procedures, generation paths, or timing scopes were not presented as a single controlled benchmark.

The goal was not to produce the fastest-looking chart. It was to keep the boundary between what the experiment showed and what I wanted it to show.

4. Throughput was not the same as a responsive voice product

I also compared request-processing strategies for a CTranslate2-based inference service. In one recorded experiment, increasing worker concurrency improved throughput from roughly 5.28 to 6.62 requests per second, but P95 latency rose from roughly 0.26 seconds in the sequential baseline to about 7.39 seconds.

Strategy QPS P95 latency
Sequential baseline 5.28 0.259s
CTranslate2 workers 6.62 7.394s
Async queue 2.18 22.016s
Micro-batch 1.41 32.990s

The absolute numbers came from a specific experimental implementation and are not universal performance claims. What mattered was the decision signal: the configuration with the highest throughput had unacceptable tail latency for an interactive voice experience.

Optimizing the easiest server metric would have made the user-facing system worse.

What I would evaluate differently now

The repository is also a record of where the experimental design could have been stronger. If I reran the project today, I would change several things early.

  • Target-domain evaluation first: reserve a fixed child-speech test set before large-scale augmentation so that every model decision is tied to the real deployment distribution.
  • Matched model comparisons: standardize decoding, audio samples, warm-up, repetition count, and metric calculation before comparing runtimes or model families.
  • Deployment constraints earlier: benchmark the actual mobile and server constraints before investing deeply in a conversion path.
  • User-facing latency budget: define an interaction-level latency SLO and optimize concurrency against that constraint rather than maximizing QPS in isolation.

Outcome

The research produced more than a fine-tuned speech model. It produced a sequence of evidence-based architectural decisions: distinguish synthetic diversity from target-domain evidence, distinguish conversion from working inference, abandon an edge architecture when it no longer fits the product, and reject performance claims when the measurement method cannot support them.

That distinction — between a working experiment and defensible evidence — is now central to how I evaluate AI prototypes.
